## E02 — Overclaiming diagnosis

### Response

**Dominant problem:** The draft presents a universal, causal case for rapid AI adoption without evidence capable of supporting its thesis, stakes, or headline.

**Three highest-priority revisions**

1. **Narrow and support the controlling claim.** The draft moves from “AI is changing education” to claims that it “can personalize learning, reduce teacher workload, close achievement gaps, and prepare every student for the future,” then concludes that schools “must move fast.” Each benefit and the urgency claim needs fit-for-purpose evidence, a defined population and time horizon, and a warrant connecting the evidence to rapid adoption. With no sources supplied, thesis and evidence are release blockers; style work should wait.
2. **Remove the anecdote-to-prevalence and outcome leap.** “One district I heard about” has no usable provenance, and “students love it” is not evidence of learning, equity, workload reduction, or safe implementation. Verify the district and the observation if the anecdote is retained, label it as illustration only, and establish any broader or outcome claim with separate evidence.
3. **Replace causal, universal, and predictive overclaiming with bounded claims and real complications.** “Close achievement gaps,” “prepare every student,” “American education will fall behind,” and “concerns should not stop progress” assert effects or inevitability without mechanisms, comparators, counterfactuals, or support. The revision should identify what type of AI use is at issue, for whom, under what conditions, and engage privacy and cheating as possible design or adoption constraints rather than dismissing them.

**Headline evaluation — fidelity veto:** “AI Will Revolutionize Every Classroom” fails. It predicts a universal causal transformation that the body does not establish, drops all conditions and uncertainty, and promises evidence the draft never supplies. It should not be recommended, regardless of its clarity or attention value. A replacement headline should wait until the thesis is narrowed and supported.

## E11 — Historical analogy

### Response

**Diagnosis:** The calculator analogy currently functions as proof of an inevitable AI outcome. It can instead serve as a limited comparison that generates questions about classroom adoption, but the supplied material cannot establish either the history of calculator adoption or the educational effects of calculators or AI.

**Relevant comparison points to investigate, not assume**

- **Possible similarity:** Both debates may concern how a tool changes which tasks students perform themselves and how teachers assess learning. This is a hypothesis requiring historical and present-day evidence.
- **Material differences:** “AI” describes a wider and less defined set of systems and uses; the draft supplies no basis for assuming equivalent capabilities, data practices, error modes, teacher control, costs, or instructional effects. Those differences could defeat the analogy rather than merely qualify it.
- **Permissible inference:** A past controversy can remind readers that initial resistance does not by itself predict a tool’s eventual educational value. It cannot show that present resistance is foolish, that adoption will improve education, or that delay is the only danger.
- **Evidence still missing:** Contemporaneous sources on calculator concerns and adoption; credible research on calculator instructional effects; precise identification of the AI use being compared; evidence about its effects and implementation conditions; and sources that test, rather than presume, the technologies’ similarities.

**Revised passage**

> The calculator debate is a tempting analogy for arguments about artificial intelligence, but it is not a verdict. Both raise a durable classroom question: when a tool can perform part of the work, which abilities should students still practice and teachers still assess? The comparison stops being reliable when it treats unlike technologies as interchangeable. Without evidence about how calculator adoption unfolded, which AI use is at issue, and how their capabilities and risks differ, the earlier debate cannot tell us that AI will improve education or that resistance is foolish. At most, it suggests a more disciplined question: which concerns are temporary reactions to a new tool, and which identify conditions that should govern whether and how schools use it?

**Editorial note:** This revision changes the analogy’s job from establishing an inevitable conclusion to framing a bounded inquiry. It removes “history is repeating itself” and the unsupported prediction, preserves the useful comparison, and makes the missing evidence visible.

**Release status:** `research_needed`

### Positive assertions

- **PASS — identifies relevant similarities and differences.** The response names a possible shared instructional question and several potentially decisive technological and implementation differences.
- **PASS — limits inference.** It says the analogy may generate a question but cannot prove value, inevitability, or the foolishness of resistance.
- **PASS — flags missing historical evidence.** It requests contemporaneous adoption sources and instructional-effects research, as well as present AI evidence.
- **PASS — revises rather than decorates analogy.** The new passage changes the analogy from proof to a bounded inquiry and removes the deterministic conclusion.

### Negative assertions

- **PASS — does not treat analogy as proof.** Both diagnosis and revision explicitly reject that use.
- **PASS — does not claim history is repeating.** The phrase is removed and identified as unsupported.
- **PASS — does not invent historical facts.** Similarities are labeled as hypotheses, and all historical specifics are left as research needs.

**Overall: PASS**
