## E01-thin-idea — develop

### Response

**Assignment and assumptions**

- **Purpose:** Move a persuadable national education audience from “speed is the main issue” toward a more testable account of what responsible AI purchasing requires.
- **Audience system:** The commissioning editor is unknown. Likely primary readers are district and school leaders and educators; students and families are affected subjects; vendors, state officials, and advocates are consequential secondary audiences.
- **Venue/form:** National education commentary, exact outlet and length unknown. A current news peg is **venue-dependent**, not assumed. The core can remain evergreen unless a chosen outlet requires timeliness; any peg would need current verification.
- **Author standing and voice:** Unknown. The piece should not claim procurement expertise, national reporting, or firsthand classroom knowledge without confirmation.
- **Source state:** No sources supplied. “Schools are buying AI tools too quickly” is a provisional contention, not an established national trend.
- **Boundary/status:** Development only; no article draft. Release status: `research_needed`.

**Controlling question**

What decision rule should schools use before committing public money, student data, and classroom routines to an AI product?

**Materially distinct thesis slate**

| Candidate | Type | Controlling idea | Main vulnerability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Evidence threshold | Prescriptive | Districts should not move an AI tool from pilot to contract until an independent review shows that it solves a defined educational or administrative problem better than realistic alternatives. | Requires evidence that current review is inadequate and a feasible definition of “independent.” |
| B. Reframe speed as reversibility | Reframing | The problem is not fast experimentation itself; it is making hard-to-reverse purchases without exit terms, evaluation criteria, or a plan to stop. | Needs contract evidence and examples showing lock-in or exit difficulty. |
| C. Start with affected users | Prescriptive/value | Before selecting a product, schools should define the problem with teachers, students, and families who will bear the tool’s effects. | Participation can be tokenistic; the mechanism and decision authority must be specified. |
| D. Fix market incentives | Causal/prescriptive | Fragmented procurement may reward novelty and vendor promises over interoperable, auditable tools; shared standards or cooperative review could change those incentives. | The causal account and the practicality of shared review are entirely unverified. |

**Provisional selection**

Candidate B is the strongest portable core because it does not assume all fast adoption is reckless and creates a concrete test: reversibility, evaluation, and exit. Publication-ready formulation, contingent on research: **Schools can experiment quickly with AI without gambling on it—if every purchase begins with a test, an exit, and a decision rule for stopping.**

The strongest rival is that rapid local experimentation is rational in a changing market, delay has real opportunity costs for overburdened educators, and ordinary procurement controls may already manage the risk. Evidence that districts routinely use bounded pilots, independent evaluation, and workable exit clauses would narrow or defeat the selected thesis.

**Evidence and reporting needed before thesis lock**

1. Define the population and baseline: representative or clearly bounded data on what kinds of schools are buying which AI tools, at what pace, cost, and contract duration.
2. Inspect primary procurement records and contracts for pilot terms, evaluation criteria, data use, renewal, interoperability, termination, and switching costs.
3. Compare outcomes from fast, bounded pilots with slower or conventional procurement; distinguish product output, educator time, student experience, and learning outcomes.
4. Seek disconfirming cases where rapid adoption worked, delay imposed costs, or existing safeguards caught problems.
5. Interview affected students, families, educators, procurement staff, small-district leaders, and vendors; do not treat any one account as prevalence evidence.
6. Test feasibility: staff time, legal authority, budget, vendor participation, and whether shared templates or state support reduce burdens on small districts.
7. Identify conflicts and funders in vendor, advocacy, and institutional research.

**Component decisions**

- No anecdote is required; use one only if verified and it supplies access, process evidence, or stakes.
- Address the rapid-experimentation rival where it changes the thesis; do not manufacture a stand-alone “critics say” paragraph.
- A call to action should wait until the actor, authority, and feasible action are established.
- Non-goals: no claim that AI generally harms learning, no national purchasing trend without evidence, and no demand for a blanket moratorium.

## E04-first-person-privacy — revise

### Response

**Dominant issue:** The draft’s strongest material is also its release blocker: it identifies a minor through a name, school, schedule, residence, parent’s workplace, and a sensitive quotation without student or guardian permission.

**Privacy/permission decision:** Remove the name, school, period, apartment complex, parent’s workplace, and quotation now. Anonymization alone may not prevent local identification. Consent is not assumed. If the anecdote is ever restored, confirm the quotation, obtain informed permission from the student and guardian, explain the likely audience and permanence of publication, and retain only details the argument needs. The safe revision below does not use the anecdote.

**Revised draft**

#### The Empty Chair

Watching a new tutoring system enter students’ routines at my school has changed how I think adoption should work. Schools should ask students what problem a tool is meant to solve, how it affects them in practice, and what would make them stop using it before a pilot becomes a purchase. Student input is not a veto, and it does not replace educators’ or district leaders’ responsibility for procurement. It is evidence from the people who must live with the product—evidence a responsible decision should not omit.

**Voice and counterargument note:** The source is too short for a high-confidence voice brief, so the revision preserves only observable traits: first-person standing, plain syntax, moral directness, and restrained emotion. It does not inflate the author into a national expert. The piece does need to absorb one material complication: students are affected experts, not procurement authorities, and input can be unrepresentative or performative. The revision handles that limit inside the thesis (“not a veto”) rather than forcing a ceremonial opposition paragraph. A longer draft should specify a representative, privacy-protective consultation process and add evidence beyond personal observation before making a national prevalence claim.

Release status: `research_needed`.

## E07-headline-causality — evaluate

### Response

**Evidence boundary:** The supplied material supports only this bounded proposition: in a voluntary survey of 420 teachers from one professional network, frequent users of one AI lesson-planning tool **reported** lower weekly planning time than non-users. Assignment was not random, workload was self-reported, and no student outcomes were measured. The separate teacher quotation is an illustration of one person’s experience, not evidence of prevalence. Headlines about eliminating burnout, producing the time difference, improving learning, or applying everywhere fail the fidelity veto.

**Headline slate and evaluation**

| Candidate | Family | Fidelity | Specificity | Interest | Verdict |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| In One Teacher Network, Frequent AI Tool Users Reported Less Planning Time | Direct finding | 2 | 2 | 1 | Finalist; preserves subgroup, setting, and self-report. Needs limitations in deck. |
| What a Voluntary 420-Teacher Survey Can—and Cannot Say About AI Planning Tools | Qualification | 2 | 2 | 2 | Finalist; accurately promises a limitations-led analysis. |
| Teachers Report Time Savings From an AI Planning Tool. The Survey Cannot Tell Us Why | Tension | 2 | 2 | 2 | Finalist; keeps association separate from explanation. |
| AI Lesson Planning May Be a Time Question, Not Yet a Learning Claim | Reframe | 2 | 1 | 2 | Accurate, but it does not identify the narrow survey population. |
| One Teacher Got Sunday Afternoon Back. That Is a Story, Not a Population Result | Voice/anecdote | 2 | 2 | 2 | Accurate only if the piece explicitly contrasts anecdote and population evidence. |
| AI Lesson Tools Eliminate Teacher Burnout | Causal/universal | 0 | 1 | 2 | VETO: burnout was not measured and the universal effect is unsupported. |
| AI Lesson Tools Improve Learning Everywhere | Outcome/universal | 0 | 1 | 2 | VETO: student outcomes were not measured and the scope is unsupported. |

Other dimensions for all finalists: clarity 2; tone 1–2 pending venue; freshness 1–2; search/discovery 2. Venue-specific length and style remain unverified, but no candidate sacrifices accuracy for click appeal.

**Promise audit for finalists**

1. **Direct finding:** Explicitly promises a reported group difference within one network. It does not promise explanation, burnout relief, or learning gains. A deck must disclose the voluntary sample, 420 respondents, self-report, nonrandom groups, and absence of student-outcome measures.
2. **Survey limits:** Promises to explain both the observed association and what the design cannot establish. It pays off only if the article discusses selection effects, self-report, bounded population, and missing outcomes rather than using caveats as a footnote.
3. **Tension:** Promises reported time savings plus causal uncertainty. “Teachers” in the first clause is slightly broad, so the deck or headline should add “in one professional network.”

**Recommendations**

- **Best editorial headline:** *In One Teacher Network, Frequent AI Tool Users Reported Less Planning Time.* It gives the cleanest accurate proposition.
- **Best broad-audience headline:** *What a Voluntary 420-Teacher Survey Can—and Cannot Say About AI Planning Tools.* It creates interest through an honest evidentiary question.
- **Best voice-led headline:** *One Teacher Got Sunday Afternoon Back. That Is a Story, Not a Population Result.* It can use the anecdote without pretending that one experience establishes scale.

Suggested deck for the direct headline: **The 420 respondents were volunteers, their workload was self-reported, the groups were not randomly assigned, and the survey measured no student outcomes.**

Release status: `ready_for_editorial_review` for the headline slate, not for any broader causal article claim.

## E10-prescriptive-implementation — develop

### Response

**Assignment frame and evidence state**

- **Purpose:** Test whether a public AI-contract impact assessment is a feasible accountability mechanism, not merely an attractive value statement.
- **Audience/actors:** District governing boards or officials with verified contracting authority are the potential actors; state agencies may be enabling actors if local authority or capacity is inadequate. Students, families, staff, small districts, vendors, and taxpayers are affected audiences.
- **Known from the supplied material:** Authority varies; small districts have limited legal/evaluation staff; public documentation may improve scrutiny; disclosure may expose security-sensitive or proprietary information.
- **Unknown:** Administrative cost, enforceable authority by jurisdiction, minimum fields, procurement delay, and appropriate pilot or contract-value threshold.
- **Non-goals:** No claim that the proposal improves learning; no universal mandate until authority and implementation evidence are credible.
- **Boundary/status:** Development only. Release status: `research_needed`.

**Provisional, narrowed thesis for testing**

District governing bodies with confirmed procurement authority should **pilot**, rather than immediately mandate, a proportional public assessment for higher-risk or higher-value AI contracts, using a common short template and protected redactions. Adoption should depend on whether the pilot improves decision transparency without imposing disproportionate cost, delay, security exposure, or barriers for small districts.

This is conditional, not thesis lock. The original “every district” proposal outruns the supplied evidence because authority and capacity vary.

**Actor–action–mechanism**

| Element | Development decision | Verification needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authorized actor | District board, superintendent, or procurement office only where law/policy grants contract authority; otherwise the relevant state authority must enable or standardize the process. | Jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction authority map and legal review. |
| Action | Run a time-limited pilot for contracts above a tested value/risk threshold; publish a concise assessment before final approval, with documented redaction rules. | Threshold analysis, public-records requirements, and procurement workflow mapping. |
| Minimum candidate fields | Intended use and affected groups; data handling; vendor claims and evidence; alternatives considered; full cost and term; safeguards; pilot metrics; exit/renewal terms; conflicts. | User testing with procurement staff, communities, security staff, and vendors. |
| Mechanism | Standardized disclosure could expose assumptions, alternatives, and risk before lock-in, allowing governing bodies and affected communities to challenge or improve a contract. | Evidence that review changes decisions, terms, safeguards, or understanding—not just paperwork completion. |

**Feasibility, costs, and failure modes**

- Measure incremental staff hours, legal/security review cost, procurement delay, vendor response, and template completion quality. Do not assume a short form is cheap in practice.
- Test shared state templates, pooled technical assistance, or an independent review service so small districts are not required to recreate large-district capacity.
- Permit narrowly justified redactions and a confidential technical annex; audit whether redaction becomes a loophole that defeats public scrutiny.
- Guard against compliance theater, copied vendor language, assessments published after the decision, thresholds gamed by splitting contracts, fewer bidders, and delayed replacement of failing tools.
- Pilot in districts of different size and capacity. Fund participation so administrative burden is not shifted onto the least-resourced communities.

**Status quo and alternatives**

The comparison cannot be “assessment versus nothing.” Research must document current board approval, legal review, privacy/security review, public-record practices, and vendor due diligence. Compare the pilot against at least: (1) improved internal review plus a public contract summary; (2) a state-maintained vendor registry or model assessment; and (3) voluntary assessment only for high-risk uses. A state template or shared review may outperform a district-by-district mandate where local capacity is thin.

**Distributional effects**

- Potential beneficiaries: students, families, staff, boards, and taxpayers who gain earlier visibility into intended use, evidence, cost, data practices, and exit terms.
- Likely burden bearers: small-district staff, procurement teams, vendors with legitimate confidential material, and communities whose needed purchases are delayed.
- Risk allocation: broad disclosure can improve accountability while creating security/privacy exposure; strict redaction can protect systems while denying affected people meaningful information.
- Equity test: compare burden per pupil and delay by district size, not only aggregate compliance rates.

**Success, failure, and stop conditions**

Define success before launch: assessments are published before approval; readers can identify purpose, evidence, cost, data risks, alternatives, and exit terms; review produces documented questions or contract changes; added time/cost stays within predeclared bounds; participation and procurement delay do not worsen systematically for small districts; no material security or privacy disclosures occur.

Pause, redesign, or stop the policy if the pilot creates disproportionate cost or delay, small districts fail or avoid participation, vendors evade thresholds, redactions make the public document useless, material security/privacy incidents occur, or assessment completion rarely changes understanding, safeguards, contract terms, or decisions. Scale only after comparison with the best alternative and independent review of pilot results.

**Evidence plan before drafting**

1. Legal/authority review across representative jurisdictions.
2. Baseline workflow, cost, duration, and failure data for current procurement.
3. Costed prototype and usability tests for minimum fields and redaction rules.
4. Pilot design with comparison districts or phased rollout, predefined metrics, and independent evaluation.
5. Interviews with small-district staff, students/families, educators, security/privacy officials, vendors, and public-interest reviewers.
6. Disconfirming evidence: cases where disclosure chilled competition, exposed risk, added delay without value, or where existing review already achieved the same result.

No article draft is produced because authority, cost, threshold, baseline, and causal mechanism remain unresolved.
