## Research synthesis

As an editorial synthesis—not a claim established by any single source—strong opinion writing coordinates five systems:

1. **Position:** a controlling insight a reasonable reader could contest.
2. **Proof:** evidence and reasoning proportionate to the claim.
3. **Reader movement:** a realistic theory of what will shift this audience.
4. **Presence:** an authorial voice and standing that matter to the argument.
5. **Form:** an opening, sequence, pace, and ending chosen for this material.

The OpEd Project's guidance offers a useful baseline—lede/news hook, argument, evidence-backed points, potential counterargument, and conclusion—but explicitly says its structure is not a rule. Preserve that caveat. Harvard's writing guidance reinforces that a thesis should be arguable, scoped, evidence-derived, and connected to stakes. Nieman Storyboard's craft interviews and annotations show why narrative structures, recurring images, deliberate point of view, and endings can carry meaning; these are options, not requirements.

Purdue's Toulmin overview grounds claim, grounds, and warrant as core functions, with backing, qualifier, and rebuttal used when needed. Harvard's counterargument guidance treats objections as reasonable alternative answers that may force thesis revision rather than as a ritual paragraph. Harvard's introduction, transition, and conclusion guidance supports problem-and-stakes orientation, old-to-new logical movement, and endings that move through what, so what, and now what without requiring action.

## Exemplar analysis protocol

When the user names a venue, field, or set of admired writers, build a small current corpus before prescribing venue craft:

1. Select 6–12 pieces comparable in venue, form, length, topic function, and audience—not only pieces with similar politics.
2. Record publication date, headline/deck, word count, news peg, author standing, opening move, thesis location/type, evidence types, paragraph jobs, treatment of objections, voice markers, pacing shifts, and ending move.
3. Distinguish a **recurring convention** (appears across multiple pieces) from a **writer-specific move** and a **single-piece flourish**.
4. Identify negative cases: successful pieces that omit anecdotes, explicit calls to action, counterargument paragraphs, or current news pegs.
5. Extract transferable functions, not surface imitation. Example: “open with a small institutional absurdity that reveals the larger claim,” not “sound like [living columnist].”
6. Confirm current venue requirements from the venue itself.
7. Summarize the design implications and uncertainties before drafting.

Respect copyright: analyze and paraphrase; do not reproduce long passages. Do not imitate a living author's signature style.

## Structural repertoire

Choose among these shapes or construct another:

- **Claim-first:** thesis → reasons/evidence → complication → implication.
- **Scene-to-system:** concrete moment → pattern → argument → return/reframe.
- **Mystery/explanation:** anomaly → competing explanations → best account → stakes.
- **Myth/reality:** dominant frame → disconfirming evidence → replacement frame.
- **Concession/pivot:** grant a strong premise → reveal what it misses → revised conclusion.
- **Scale shift:** person → institution → system → person/choice.
- **Before/after:** change over time → causal or interpretive account → future consequence.
- **Braided:** alternate two cases or lenses until their relationship becomes the thesis.

Do not select by novelty alone. Select the shape that makes the reasoning easiest to follow and the stakes hardest to ignore.

## Anti-formula principles

- One controlling idea does not require one simplistic claim.
- Clarity does not require stating the thesis in the first sentence.
- Fairness does not require symmetrical treatment of unequal evidence.
- An anecdote is not mandatory and never substitutes for scale.
- A counterargument is a reasoning function, not necessarily a paragraph.
- A call to action is not mandatory; some pieces aim to reinterpret or bear witness.
- A memorable ending need not be a slogan or callback.
- Voice is not decoration added after the argument; it is the author's way of noticing, reasoning, and valuing.
